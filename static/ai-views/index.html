Bodong Chen on AI in education

A living, curated summary of my current thinking on AI

How this page is made. This summary is drafted by an AI assistant (Claude) from Bodong Chen’s papers, talks, blog posts, and notes, then reviewed and corrected by him. It is revised as his views develop. Voice is third person, and not everything he has written appears here; omission is editorial.

As Chen puts it, “I’m less interested in whether students use AI than in who is doing the thinking, and who gets to shape the environment.”

In brief

  • Capacity comes from the learner’s own work, so AI should scaffold that work rather than substitute for it.
  • Learning needs productive friction, and AI should take on extraneous load while leaving germane load to learners.
  • Understanding lives in idiosyncratic connections, which shared models pull toward one voice.
  • Keeping AI out of learning is not always the best way to protect it, as top Go players who study AI’s moves show.
  • Learning needs human time.
  • Whether AI helps depends on how it enters the work and what the learner can already do.
  • Every decision about AI in a classroom is an architectural decision.
  • Start from pedagogy, and decide on purpose which learning objectives should change.
  • AI can propose, and people should keep the judgment.
  • Teachers should hold authority over the AI in their classrooms.
  • Education is for people’s growth as knowers and members of societies.
  • Measure the right things in education, and measure them right.

Learning and what AI changes about it

Capacity comes from the learner’s own work

Chen believes that what a learner can do without help grows out of the work the learner does, such as generating ideas, reasoning through problems, and revising drafts. AI that does that work can raise performance while the tool is present and lower it once the tool is gone. In one field experiment, high-school students given GPT-4 during practice scored 48% higher than a control group on practice problems and 17% lower on the closed-book exam that followed, while a tutor version that gave hints instead of answers removed the harm (Bastani et al., 2025). In another study, university students who revised essays with ChatGPT wrote better essays than students with a human expert, a checklist, or no support, and gained no more knowledge or transfer. Their revising also moved less often between orienting to the task and evaluating their own text, a pattern the authors call metacognitive laziness (Fan et al., 2025).

His design rule for AI in learning tools follows from this. The tool should scaffold the learner’s work rather than substitute for it.

Sources: Epistemic niche integrative review (in preparation); KBSI featured talk (March 2026); From Epistemic Infrastructure to Niche Construction: Who Builds Educational Futures with AI? (keynote, Peking University, November 2026).

Learning needs productive friction

Chen holds that learning needs friction, and that the friction worth keeping is the effort that builds understanding. Cognitive load theory calls this germane load, as when a learner generates an explanation, retrieves what they know, or works out how a new idea fits with old ones. It differs from extraneous load, the effort spent on things that do not serve learning, such as hunting for materials or fighting a confusing interface. Collaboration adds friction of its own. Students have to negotiate what they mean, take one another’s perspectives, and work through disagreement, and doing that work is part of what they learn.

Minimizing friction can be the central goal for commercial products such as social media, which are built to maximize engagement. For learning tools it is not always the right goal. Many AI products for learners remove the friction through which learning happens, because removing it gives learners what they want in the moment, whether the answer, the finished essay, or the fast completion. The frictionless default is the easy path for builders, for understandable reasons. It satisfies what users want right now, and the cost falls on their future selves. He leaves open which dynamic does the most damage over time: assistance creeping from the margins of a task to its core, dependency, assignments and grading reorganizing around AI use, or rising expectations for polish and volume.

His design rule is to let AI absorb extraneous load and leave germane load, including the work of collaborating, to learners. CraftPad, his lab’s tool for teachers designing lessons, also follows this rule. AI retrieves materials, organizes scattered ideas, and formats the plan, while teachers reason about the lesson’s purposes, how students will think, and which instructional choices to make.

Sources: PKU keynote (November 2026); Notes (2026).

Idiosyncrasy in human learning

Chen believes that understanding forms when learners connect new ideas to their own experiences, knowledge, and ways of thinking, which Wittrock called generative learning, and that the idiosyncrasy of those connections matters for learning with understanding. He worries about what happens when students’ writing drifts toward a model’s vocabulary and polish. Words favored by ChatGPT rose abruptly in academic abstracts after its release (Kobak et al., 2025). If students lose their distinctive ways of expressing ideas, or thinking about them at the first place, they may also lose the personal connections that make learning meaningful and transferable. He holds tool developers partly responsible, since models trained toward polished language feed a loop in which people adopt the model’s style.

Sources: Preserving Human Idiosyncrasy: Generative Learning in the Age of Generative AI (blog post, July 2025).

Reading

Chen sees reading as relationship building, the slow work of developing one’s own thinking in conversation with other people’s ideas. Tools that read for you, decide what matters, and hand over the gist turn the reader into a consumer of pre-digested insight. He contrasts this with vibe coding, where the person still sets directions and tests results. With vibe reading, the reader risks dropping out of their own learning. His research on reading predates generative AI and includes studies of social annotation, where students read a text together and discuss it in the margins (Zhu et al., 2020).

His lab’s InkSpire project uses AI to help instructors guide students to read academically, in the ways their discipline reads and reasons. Instructors describe their discipline, course, and students, and InkSpire drafts reading scaffolds, which are prompts tied to particular passages. It chooses the passages by cognitive load and by the discipline’s core practices. Instructors accept, edit, or decline each scaffold. Students do not use the AI themselves. They see the curated scaffolds in Perusall as instructor annotations beside the passages, where the scaffolds become starting points for discussing the text (Liang, Wang, & Chen, 2026). Pilots in neuroscience and materials science courses are under way.

Sources: From Vibe Coding to Vibe Reading (Substack, 2025); Reading and Connecting: Using Social Annotation in Online Classes (Zhu et al., 2020); Liquid Margins 15: Social Annotation, Bridging Theory and Practice (Hypothesis webinar he moderated, December 2020); InkSpire: An LLM-Powered System for Designing and Generating Disciplinarily Aligned and Context-Aware Reading Scaffolds (Liang, Wang, & Chen, Intelligent Textbooks workshop, 2026); InkSpire project page.

Learning from and with AI at the frontier

Chen argues that keeping AI out of learning is not always the best way to protect it. His example is professional Go. After superhuman Go programs arrived, the quality of professional players’ moves rose after decades of little change, and novel moves became more frequent and more often good (Shin et al., 2023). The gains were uneven. Lee Sedol, whose 2016 match against AlphaGo marked the start of this period, has said he expected AI to lift all players to one standard and saw the opposite, with top-ranked players pulling ahead because they used and understood AI better (Lee, 2026). By Lee’s estimate, a player who understands even three of every ten moves an AI plays can reach the top ranks, and years of studying AI raised his own understanding until he began playing a human style of Go again.

Chen reads this as a story about intentional effort to learn from and with AI. Top players studied AI’s moves, made sense of them, and brought them into their own repertoire, while others memorized the moves or did not study them closely. Chess offers a partial parallel, where chess engines (like Stockfish) helped players improve by standing in for scarce human sparring partners but did not fully replace training with people (Gaessler & Piezunka, 2023).

He proposes adding this kind of learning from and with AI to what students already learn. AI could keep stretching the zone of proximal development of a learner or a class, as it stretched Go players. If education can design for this, refusing AI has an opportunity cost, especially in courses meant to train domain experts.

Sources: Notes (November 2026); Lee Sedol’s lecture at the FKI CEO Jeju Summer Forum, as reported by FNNews (July 2026); Lee Sedol at the AI World 2026 panel, as reported by Financial News (September 2026, in Korean).

Human scale and well-being

AI lag

Chen believes that learning needs human time. Once AI raises the expected speed of some kind of work, people collectively can’t slow back down, and the mismatch between machine time and human time shows up as exhaustion, which he calls AI lag. He argues for deliberately choosing when to work at machine speed and when to keep a human pace, including slow zones protected from AI acceleration. His own practice is to do squats while an AI model finishes a task he has handed it, to stay grounded and keep a human scale. He thinks AI literacy programs should take the clash between machine time and human time seriously, and he warns that the freedom to stay on human time may become a new form of inequality.

Sources: From Jet Lag to AI Lag (Substack, 2025); AI Squats (2026).

Knowing together, from classrooms to societies

Knowing in a community

Chen comes to AI from Knowledge Building, where students work as a community to improve ideas and take collective responsibility for what they come to know. He argues that AI changes the conditions of that work, including how a community checks claims. Fluent output can pass unchecked unless a community holds claims to a shared standard. Mathematicians keep one. They may disagree about which mathematics is interesting, but they broadly agree on what counts as a correct and complete proof, and a proof written in a proof assistant such as Lean can be checked by computer. The Equational Theories Project accepted both human and automated arguments, since each could be checked, and settled more than 22 million true-or-false statements in universal algebra (Tao, 2026). Designs can also keep people doing the checking. When AI feedback was routed through conference reviewers instead of replacing them, 26.6% of reviewers revised their reviews, against 9.4% in a control group (Thakkar et al., 2026).

Sources: KBSI featured talk (March 2026); Epistemic niche integrative review (in preparation); Math 2.0 (Terence Tao, Caltech talk, 2026).

Interaction patterns in using AI

Chen believes that whether AI helps depends on how it enters the work and on what the learner can already do, so rules that only allow or ban AI miss what matters. With colleagues he proposed situating AI use within interaction patterns drawn from knowledge-building dialogue (Chen, Zhu, & Díaz del Castillo H., 2023). In a high school world religions course in Colombia, students used ChatGPT early in an inquiry to open up the problem and later to challenge their emerging ideas. AI made the learning harder and deeper. Students used it for inspiration, not answers, and collaborated more with their peers.

He thinks of AI as a flashlight rather than a magic wand. A flashlight helps someone who has already developed the night vision to judge what it shows, and for someone still developing that capacity it can shine too bright, too soon. Interaction structure also shapes a group’s work. With the same model and task, AI acting as a structured critic made people’s work converge, while AI that offered opening proposals and then stepped back produced more varied work (Najjar & Nasir, 2026). Across the four feedback mechanisms in his integrative review, the direction of each traced back to how AI entered the work, such as how much of the work it took over and whether people kept doing the checking.

Sources: Integrating generative AI in knowledge building (2023); Enhancing Student and Teacher Agency in Knowledge Building with AI (talk, Singapore, November 2025); From Magic Wand to Flashlight (Substack, 2025); PKU keynote (November 2026).

Variation across people and traditions

Chen argues that shared models can narrow the range of ideas, and that the narrowing has a direction. When many people draw on the same models, each contribution can improve while the set becomes more alike. Stories written with AI ideas were rated more novel one by one and more similar to each other as a set (Doshi & Hauser, 2024), and AI suggestions moved Indian writers toward Western styles (Agarwal et al., 2025). He wants AI for learning to protect variation across classrooms and knowledge traditions, the aim behind Yuk Hui’s idea of technodiversity. In his reading, China’s AI in education shares the West’s datafying, optimizing image of technology and differs mainly in the political form of its deployment. For him, technodiversity in education remains a horizon rather than a fact.

Sources: Book chapter on AI in Chinese education (in preparation); Epistemic niche integrative review (in preparation); PKU keynote (November 2026).

Building learning environments

Infrastructure for knowing and learning

Chen holds that what people know rests on infrastructure they did not build and rarely see. A weather forecast rests on more than 150 years of shared observation, data exchange, and modeling (Edwards, 2010). Education rests on the same kinds of parts, from teachers, students, and parents to textbooks, exams, learning platforms, examination authorities, and the norms by which a society recognizes good teaching and evidence of understanding. Infrastructure is built for some purpose, and its users are expected to take it for granted until it breaks down (Star, 1999). When Google’s AI Overviews advised adding glue to pizza sauce, drawing on a joke posted to Reddit, the breakdown showed what sat under the answer.

Every infrastructure is optimized for some goal, and the goal is not always its users’ interest. Algorithmic feeds are tuned to hold attention. On social media, false news spread faster than true news (Vosoughi et al., 2018), and people rewarded with likes for outrage posted more of it (Brady et al., 2021). Chen calls an information environment optimized for attention instead of justified belief epistemic hijacking, and AI built by the same companies, or on the same commercial logic, could repeat the pattern. A forecast earns trust because it is scored every day against the weather that came, while a feed is scored by the attention it holds. AI in education needs a standard of its own. He would check it against what learners can do later without the tool, and against how varied and how well checked a community’s ideas remain.

Sources: PKU keynote (November 2026).

Every decision is architectural

Chen believes that decisions about AI in a classroom are architectural. When a student asked whether they could use ChatGPT to outline a paper, he thought he was answering a question. He was designing the infrastructure of his course. Every course is an environment for thinking, and AI is now part of the material it is built from. Tools carry epistemologies, so choosing a tool also chooses what counts as knowing in that room, and what gets built passes to the next cohort as its starting point. He calls the process by which communities build the conditions of their knowledge work, and are shaped by them, epistemic niche construction. The question he now asks of each decision is what kind of knowing he is building infrastructure for.

Two schools show how differently a niche can be built. At a Knowledge Building lab school in Toronto, one Grade 4 teacher redesigned how his class worked over three years, moving from fixed groups to teams that formed whenever the class’s questions called for them, and the third design brought the most knowledge advances (Zhang et al., 2009). At Alpha School in Austin, students spend about two hours a day on adaptive apps that choose each student’s next lesson, and adults work as guides. The two schools differ in what counts as worth knowing and in how knowledge gets built, and every AI tool a school adopts now enters a niche like one of these. He argues that educational futures in the AI era rest on people acting as constructors of their epistemic niches rather than as passive adopters of infrastructure built for them.

The environments that shape what students know also extend beyond school. Libraries and museums are slow and curated, social media feeds are built for engagement, and AI chatbots are helpful but frictionless. He wants teachers and students to have a hand in shaping these environments, within the classroom and outside it.

Sources: Reclaiming Agency: Educators as Architects of AI-Enhanced Learning Architectures (keynote, Language Educator Symposium, December 2025); KBSI featured talk (March 2026); Rethinking Epistemic Agency (KB Connects, April 2026); Who’s Doing the Thinking? (September 2026); PKU keynote (November 2026).

Design principles

From his review of how AI enters learning, Chen draws five design principles. Each acts on one or two of the feedback mechanisms the review identifies, and he treats each as a hypothesis for design research.

  1. Scaffold construction rather than substitute for it. Learners attempt the work first and AI helps after, as in the hint-only tutor, with support fading as understanding grows.
  2. Support meta-representational capacity. AI produces maps of a community’s ideas, showing how they connect, where inquiry has stalled, and how similar contributions have become, and learners do the interpreting and judge which ideas are promising.
  3. Make AI’s epistemic dispositions visible and contestable. Designs keep retrieved evidence apart from generated synthesis and show uncertainty that matches the model’s confidence, so learners can see where a claim comes from and question it.
  4. Protect variation across epistemic niches. Learners generate their own ideas before they see AI suggestions, and models are grounded in a community’s own knowledge instead of generic output.
  5. Design for revisable inheritance. Teachers log the AI-era changes they make to course documents and put expiry dates on prompts and templates, so that continuing to use them becomes a decision.

His lab is trying these principles in its own designs, without findings yet. In WonderBits, teachers decide which AI agents take part in a class, how active they are, and when they are present. Buzz reports point out gaps in a class’s ideas, such as claims without evidence, and leave the reflecting to students. CraftPad represents the knowledge and reasoning behind each lesson plan so teachers can examine and revise it.

Sources: PKU keynote (November 2026); Epistemic niche integrative review (in preparation).

Pedagogy first

Chen reads AI adoption in higher education as bottom-up and uneven. Students moved first, institutions followed with detection and bans, and pedagogy is still catching up, with AI on the teaching side drawing far less attention. Most of what has happened so far drops AI into existing practices, and he is more interested in what practices to design around it. He believes such design should start from pedagogy, not technology, and that the same principles can lead to different designs. His own principles are Dewey’s view that education is life itself rather than preparation for it, and authentic disciplinary practice, which lets students practice what their field is becoming, research included.

In two of his courses the same principles led to different decisions. In programming fundamentals the learning objectives changed, and students now work with GitHub Copilot on authentic problems, with the focus moved from syntax to problem solving and reading code critically. In learning theories the objectives stayed the same, and ChatLab helps students connect the theories to their own interests. He asks instructors to decide on purpose which objectives should change, and to model their own use of AI, including where they choose not to use it.

His course policy rests on responsibility and transparency. Students may use AI to supplement their own critical thinking but not to substitute for it, and they say when and where they used it. He prefers transparency to detection, since detectors are unreliable and policing pushes AI use underground. He also asks instructors to name unequal access to paid tools and to the know-how for using them well, and to use tools the institution provides where possible.

Sources: Purposeful AI Use: Same Principles, Different Design Outcomes (CETLI faculty workshop, Penn, September 2025); Who’s Doing the Thinking? (September 2026).

Teaching expertise

Chen argues that teacher-facing AI can substitute for the work through which teaching expertise forms. Teachers develop judgment by reading student work, planning lessons, and assessing whether a lesson worked. When a tool performs those operations, invites teachers to accept its output, and gets used repeatedly, it builds deference in place of judgment, a pattern he calls epistemic substitution. Lesson-plan generators also automate older pedagogy. AI-generated lesson plans defaulted to teacher-centered instruction with scripted question-and-answer dialogue (Cheng, Wang, & Chen, 2025).

Sources: Beyond Tools: Generative AI as Epistemic Infrastructure in Education (arXiv preprint, 2025); Reclaiming Agency keynote (December 2025).

Educators and students as builders

Chen believes the best AI tools for learning have not been built yet, and that educators and students should help build them. Most AI in schools is built by companies with their own goals, and educators and learners are rarely at the design table. Educators know things about learning that tool engineers don’t, and AI now lets them express that craft in tools of their own. He tells graduate students to build tools, not only adopt them, since a doctoral student can now prototype a small learning tool in a weekend.

His own experiments include ChatLab for his learning theories class, CraftPad for teachers designing lessons, Pip and Buzz in WonderBits, and InkSpire for instructors scaffolding disciplinary reading, each starting from learning goals and keeping students and teachers doing the thinking. CraftPad treats teachers as designers of Knowledge Building lessons, not consumers of generated plans. Of 52 teachers who designed lessons with it, many revised beyond the first version, and some made up to 96 revisions of a single lesson.

He sees students as builders too. Students who understand how learning works can judge whether a tool helps them learn, and building lets them act on that judgment. With Knowledge Building colleagues he has explored vibe coding as a way for secondary students and their teachers to build tools of their own.

Sources: Reclaiming Agency keynote (December 2025); Enhancing Student and Teacher Agency in Knowledge Building with AI (Singapore, November 2025); Rethinking Epistemic Agency (KB Connects, April 2026); Who’s Doing the Thinking? (September 2026); Notes (2026).

Responsibility and reach

Chen holds that everyone who builds learning environments shares responsibility for them, and that responsibility grows with reach. Product teams, platform providers, policymakers, teachers, and researchers all build parts of the environments learners inherit. A product default becomes the starting condition of every classroom that adopts it, and AI companies have the most reach. He criticizes design patterns rather than products and pairs each critique with a redesign, because the reach that spreads a frictionless default can spread a better one. Assessment decides which learning objectives count, new or enduring, and it is the structure later cohorts inherit most. What researchers measure becomes what gets designed for, as time on the app is what a feed is designed for. Learners build too, and he wants designs that help them construct their own epistemic niches on purpose.

Sources: PKU keynote (November 2026).

Authority over AI

Epistemic agency

Chen sees the central tension in AI for learning as a question of agency, about who does the thinking and who designs the environment where it happens. He distinguishes two kinds of control over belief (Hieronymi, 2009). Managerial control is agency over the work around forming a belief, such as choosing what to read or how to organize collaboration. Evaluative control is agency in settling for oneself, in light of reasons, what is true. Knowledge Building has mostly cultivated the managerial kind, with students taking on decisions about long-range goals, inquiry strategies, social arrangements, and criteria of success (Scardamalia, 2002). He argues that evaluative control, and intellectual virtues such as curiosity and humility that support it, need more attention now that language models produce confident prose through opaque processes.

The same AI, he argues, can cultivate or substitute for epistemic agency depending on what surrounds it (Cassinadri, 2024). If a class asks AI which of its notes are most promising and accepts the ranking, the judgment moves to the model, and the class’s own capacity to judge promise can atrophy. If the class asks AI to turn a discussion into an argument map, the output enters the discourse as new material, and the community keeps the judgment. His advice to learners, teachers, and researchers alike is to let AI propose and keep the decision, and to practice rejecting outputs and saying why. In his lab’s WonderBits, AI assistants suggest connections between students’ ideas and students accept or reject them, so each decision leaves a record of student judgment that teachers and researchers can examine.

Sources: Rethinking Epistemic Agency in Knowledge Building in the Age of AI (KB Connects, April 2026, with Yuan, Teo, Ong, Cao, and Feng); Who’s Doing the Thinking? Purposeful AI for Learners, Teachers, and Researchers (graduate panel, Penn GSE, September 2026).

Teachers

Chen believes teachers should hold authority over the AI in their classrooms. A teacher’s design, including her goals, the ideas she expects to hear, what students compose themselves, and when AI should hold back, should reach every AI tool her students use, and evidence of student thinking should come back to her in terms of that design.

Today each tool carries its developer’s idea of good teaching, and the teacher’s design does not reach it. How far commercial tools will let a teacher’s design in remains open, since many accept only settings or custom instructions.

Sources: Notes (2026); Beyond Tools (arXiv preprint, 2025).

Students

Chen argues that students should have a say in how their schools use AI. Schools rarely ask students what AI should do in their learning or invite them to help shape it. Students who study their own learning can find problems worth solving and bring recommendations on AI use to their schools.

Sources: Notes (2026).

Gradual disempowerment

Chen connects AI in schools to the risk the AI safety field calls gradual disempowerment. General-purpose AI tools are designed to complete tasks, so in classrooms they take over the generating and judging of ideas that students would otherwise do. Students in school now will be the adults living through the transition to more capable AI, and if that work keeps getting handed off, fewer of them will be able to judge and correct AI systems. He sees schools as an early case of a broader question, how communities keep authority over AI systems built by others.

Sources: Notes (2026).

Research and evidence

What studies should measure

Chen argues that evaluations of AI in education mostly measure how well individual learners perform with the tool, in a single session or a short course, and that this misses much of what AI does to learning. Evaluations at the individual level will overestimate the benefits of assistance from a shared model, because gains in quality and losses in diversity appear at different levels. He wants studies to report unassisted performance after a delay, how alike learners’ work becomes alongside its average quality, and, where time allows, changes in the practices a community uses to accept knowledge.

Sources: Epistemic niche integrative review (in preparation); PKU keynote (November 2026).

AI in research practice

Chen treats AI in research as both topic and tool. His lab uses language models to evaluate the promise of ideas in knowledge-building discourse, and he regards the validity and reproducibility of such uses as open questions, along with what still counts as the researcher’s own judgment.

He advises doctoral students to keep a short AI log of when they used AI, for what task, what it did, and what they decided. The log records their judgment and helps them answer methods questions while norms for AI in dissertation work are still forming.

Sources: Who’s Doing the Thinking? (graduate panel, Penn GSE, September 2026).

What education is for

Beyond the next list of skills

Chen thinks that even the most accomplished AI scientists can misread education when they forecast its future. Demis Hassabis has said that the most important skill for the next generation will be “learning how to learn,” so that people can keep pace as AI changes work and schooling (Hassabis, 2025). In 2026 he went further and described how he would rebuild school. He would “invert the classroom.” Students would do rote learning at home with AI that tailors it to each student’s pace, a few star teachers would give the lectures everyone uses, and class time would go to projects, group work, and Oxbridge-style tutorials (Hassabis, 2026). Little of this is new. Flipped classrooms, self-paced teaching machines, adaptive tutors, and MOOCs built around celebrated lecturers each promised something similar, with modest results (Reich, 2020), and Alpha School, described above, already runs on a similar model. Chen finds the view of learning behind the plan naive. It treats knowledge as content that individuals absorb alone and at their own speed, while understanding forms when learners connect ideas to their own experience and work them through with others. Pacing rote learning to each student individualizes it, which is different from making it personal.

The skills answer is not new either. Education has moved through lists of 21st-century skills and soft skills to today’s durable skills, and each list named what a changing economy was expected to need. The lists change with each forecast, while the answer to what education is for stays an economic one. Learning how to learn is also an old aim. Learning scientists have long studied it as metacognition and self-regulated learning, and it grows through the work of learning something in particular.

In his view, education is for people’s growth as knowers and as members of societies. That means learning to think for oneself and with others, to judge what is true, to improve ideas together, and to shape the environments that shape one in turn.

Sources: Notes (2026); Demis Hassabis in Athens, as reported by AP (September 2025); Hassabis on inverting the classroom, as reported by OfficeChai (November 2026) and, from his WCIT lecture with Wendy Hall, by The AI Corner (August 2026); Failure to Disrupt: Why Technology Alone Can’t Transform Education (Reich, 2020).

Measuring the right things, and measuring them right

Chen agrees that education needs to change, and he thinks much of that change depends on what gets measured. For decades, U.S. education has been judged mainly by K–12 test scores, and once advanced placement, dual enrollment, apprenticeships, and other postsecondary paths are counted, the dominant trend is growth rather than decline (Kirst & Chan, 2026). Narrow measures misread a system, and they also steer it, because what gets measured becomes what schools and designers work toward. If education is for people’s growth as knowers and members of societies, its measures should include how well students judge claims, how they consider one another’s ideas, and what they can do later without help.

Even the right measures can be taken badly when they track only what is easy to see. Some systems score attention from students’ faces and posture or from how often they look up at the front of the room, and remote proctoring software flags students who look away from the screen. These measures reward the appearance of attention and can mistake quiet thinking for distraction (Wang, Song, & Du, 2026), and students who know they are watched learn to perform for the measure.

Sources: A new framework for student success: Measuring what matters (School’s In podcast, Stanford GSE, October 2026); Broadening Our Perspective Concerning America’s Education Attainment: Growth, Progress, and Data Gaps (Kirst & Chan, UC Berkeley CSHE, 2026); Book chapter on AI in Chinese education (in preparation); Notes (2026).

Relational intelligence

Relational intelligence is one part of his answer. He proposes that education cultivate it, meaning the capacities to sustain relationships, exercise moral imagination, and stay connected to community and the natural world. These capacities resist automation and support human flourishing, and in his view a design can make them possible without being able to engineer them.

Sources: Relational intelligence paper (in preparation, 2026); When Relationships Are What We’re Left With (Substack, August 2025).
